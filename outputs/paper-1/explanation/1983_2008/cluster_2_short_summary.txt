The current thematic cluster in food analytics is characterized by the integration of computational and analytical techniques—particularly computer vision, chemometrics, chemoinformatics, and artificial intelligence—in the objective assessment, classification, and quantification of foods across matrices such as meat, fruit, vegetable, grain, cheese, pizza, and fish [UT 1859][UT 1851][UT 1849][UT 1857][UT 1848]. Computer vision remains central, enabling automated and repeatable imaging-based inspection that simulates human perception, while chemometric and multivariate analyses provide robust frameworks for compositional data interpretation, dietary pattern identification, and validation [UT 1868][UT 1861][UT 2099]. Recent trends highlight a shift from manual to automated, non-contact, and real-time analytic systems employing sophisticated modeling and data fusion—such as spectral interpretation and structure-activity elucidation via chemoinformatics—across a growing breadth of food products [UT 2067][UT 1864][UT 1865][UT 2117][UT 1854]. However, this rapid evolution faces notable challenges such as difficulties in accurate image segmentation under overlapping or complex arrangements, limited generalizability and robustness of AI-driven models in heterogeneous food types, lingering reproducibility issues in chemometric approaches, insufficient methodological harmonization, and concerns about the quality and coherence of food composition data [UT 395][UT 1866][UT 1857][UT 2117][UT 2099]. Research opportunities remain abundant, particularly regarding improving sensor networks, integrating multimodal imaging, refining database methodologies, and developing adaptive and hybrid algorithms for more discriminative and robust analysis—especially for complex and culturally diverse foods, or in the objective assessment of novel sensory properties [UT 1864][UT 1865][UT 1866][UT 2117][UT 1877]. To enhance practical value, it is imperative to prioritize the development of open, standardized protocols and interoperable databases, establish collaborative validation frameworks, promote cross-disciplinary benchmarking, and customize analytic workflows to both industrial and public health contexts. Collectively, these efforts will not only increase the reliability, generalizability, and reproducibility of computer-driven food analytics, but also deepen their relevance in addressing contemporary needs for transparent, efficient, and high-quality food evaluation systems [UT 1849][UT 1859][UT 1868][UT 2099].
