DEFINITION:

This cluster delineates the conceptual and methodological foundation underlying the integration of digital transformation and advanced data-driven technologies—such as big data analytics, Internet of Things (IoT), blockchain, artificial intelligence, robotics, digital twins, and machine learning—across the food and agri-food industries. Anchored in the paradigm shift associated with Industry 4.0, the cluster’s scope encompasses a broad continuum from smart agriculture and food production to processing, packaging, distribution, consumption, and waste management, fundamentally reconceptualizing food systems as interconnected, intelligent, and powerfully data-centric networks. Methodologically, it is characterized by the replacement of analog, destructive, and time-consuming techniques with automated, non-destructive, real-time, and high-throughput approaches, utilizing platforms such as smart sensors, RFID, advanced analytics, network-based architectures, and robotics [UT 1924][UT 1890][UT 139][UT 1892][UT 1456][UT 163][UT 97][UT 3][UT 772][UT 1511][UT 149][UT 524][UT 806][UT 55]. Thematic concerns span operational efficiency, supply chain transparency, traceability, safety, regulatory compliance, decision-support, and system resilience, responding to contemporary disruptors such as climate change, pandemics (including covid-19), and rapid digitalization [UT 1890][UT 364][UT 682]. The abstracts further emphasize multi-level analyses—from granular, high-resolution data flows in waste and quality analyses to large-scale, distributed, and privacy-aware collaborative networks—supported by multi-source data environments and empirical, model-driven, and bibliometric methodologies [UT 369][UT 374][UT 1353][UT 560][UT 1376][UT 601][UT 1918][UT 290][UT 1303][UT 1372]. Collectively, this cluster advances a systems-level perspective that integrates digital, big, and internet-based innovations within the industrial and agri-food sectors, foregrounding sustainability, transparency, power, traceability, and adaptability amidst dynamic global challenges.

TRENDS:

In this cluster, the literature demonstrates a pronounced and accelerating adoption of digital technologies—most notably big data, artificial intelligence, Internet of Things (IoT), and blockchain—across the agri-food and food industry value chains. A confluence of empirical and modeling studies reveals the widespread deployment of machine and deep learning models for tasks such as risk management, demand forecasting, inventory optimization, food safety, waste reduction, and precision agriculture, with recurrent evidence of superiority in neural network approaches for time-series forecasting and food quality assessment [UT 594][UT 1456][UT 1299][UT 1766][UT 1421][UT 1545][UT 306][UT 1455][UT 56][UT 364]. IoT infrastructure, including RFID, smart sensors, and autonomous robotics, has enabled real-time monitoring, digital traceability, cold-chain logistics, and automated quality control, while the operationalization of cloud and fog computing and machine-to-machine communication underpins new levels of efficiency and scalability [UT 1906][UT 1892][UT 1299][UT 254][UT 1385]. The COVID-19 outbreak has notably catalyzed the digital transformation of supply chains, intensifying applications in remote surveillance, emergency planning, and consumer-facing analytics—a trend extended through the integration of Industry 4.0, smart factory concepts, and blockchain for transparency, trust, and data security [UT 139][UT 682][UT 149][UT 125][UT 241][UT 1411][UT 1893][UT 1701][UT 1899][UT 1884]. Diversification of methods—spanning omics-driven fingerprinting, bioinformatics, digital twins, sentiment mining, and explainable AI—further reflects efforts to address climate, food security, and sustainability imperatives at scale [UT 1924][UT 120][UT 81][UT 1755][UT 1343]. The trajectory within this thematic family thus showcases a systemic shift toward data-rich, powerfully networked, and digitally integrated food systems—fundamentally reshaping industry practices, research foci, and empirical applications in response to both ongoing disruptions and long-term demands for resilience, efficiency, and consumer engagement [UT 1890][UT 1918][UT 49].

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster reveals a pervasive set of conceptual, methodological, and implementation challenges that collectively impede the digital transformation of the food industry in the era of big data, IoT, and blockchain-powered analytics. Conceptually, fragmentation persists due to divergent definitions, the lack of integrative frameworks for Industry 4.0 extensions, and inadequate consensus on standards, which undermines interoperability and the seamless integration of advanced digital technologies across agri-food and industrial supply chains [UT 560][UT 313][UT 1616]. Methodologically, the inherent complexity, heterogeneity, and fluctuating quality of food-related data—from images and sensor streams to social media signals—pose substantial burdens for data harmonization, reliable feature engineering, and the deployment of interpretable machine learning and AI models, especially under dynamic contexts such as the COVID-19 outbreak or climate-induced disruptions [UT 38][UT 209][UT 193][UT 1346][UT 214][UT 1831]. Issues of data privacy, security, ownership, and transparency further complicate collaborative analytics, while the lack of comprehensive databases and validated metrics limits robust risk assessment and sustainability evaluation [UT 1885][UT 1303][UT 49][UT 772]. From an implementation perspective, significant gaps remain in technological infrastructure, digital literacy, and systematic assimilation—challenges that are particularly acute for farmers, smaller enterprises, and resource-constrained environments [UT 1407][UT 337][UT 3]. Pandemic-induced disruptions, socio-cultural resistance, regulatory uncertainties, and the expanding digital divide exacerbate difficulties in system deployment, real-time monitoring, and automation of processes ranging from supply chain optimization to robotic intervention [UT 682][UT 1755][UT 204][UT 1238]. Finally, achieving eco-friendly and scalable digitalization is further complicated by e-waste, uneven sustainability focus, and the absence of strategic organizational and policy responses, collectively undermining the realization of a resilient and digitally empowered food sector [UT 1892][UT 560][UT 601].

OPPORTUNITIES:

In this cluster, synthesizing the identified gaps and limitations reveals a multifaceted landscape of research opportunities at the intersection of big data, digitalization, and the rapidly evolving food industry. A prominent line of inquiry involves advancing integrated, multi-technology platforms that leverage AI, IoT, robotics, blockchain, and data analytics to transform the food value chain, moving beyond siloed deployments and fostering robust digital ecosystems [UT 1890][UT 806][UT 55]. Addressing data fragmentation, standardization, and interoperability—especially through federated architectures and collaborative data-sharing environments—remains essential for unlocking the full predictive power and commercial scalability of digital food solutions [UT 1885][UT 1392][UT 209][UT 38]. The integration of omics, CRISPR, and machine learning with digital platforms, such as in 'Food Quality 4.0', holds promise for rapid, non-destructive assessment and traceability [UT 1924][UT 1376]. Methodological advancements are needed in explainable AI, multimodal data fusion, and collaborative clustering to enhance transparency, personalisation, and handling of heterogeneous data across applications spanning quality control, food safety, and consumer health management [UT 1753][UT 2028][UT 48][UT 1455]. Responding to disruptions from COVID-19 and climate change, future research should prioritize digital strategies for real-time crisis management, resilient supply chain design, and adaptive risk assessment [UT 139][UT 214][UT 1723]. Innovations in eco-friendly sensing, RFID technologies, and cloud/fog architectures can further strengthen traceability while minimizing environmental impact [UT 1892][UT 1346]. Expanding interdisciplinary research—incorporating legal, ethical, and regulatory analyses, as well as empirical studies across international contexts—will be crucial for harmonizing data governance, safeguarding privacy, and accelerating the responsible diffusion of digital tools throughout agri-food systems [UT 1303][UT 1842][UT 1422]. Collectively, these directions underscore the centrality of digital, big, and internet-powered approaches in addressing industrial, climatic, and pandemic-related challenges in modern food analytics.

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster within food analytics, it is essential to pursue strategies that support scalability, robustness, and inclusivity across digital, industrial, and agri-food contexts. Central recommendations include the development of standardized data protocols, interoperable platforms, and universal ontologies to overcome data silos and foster seamless integration of analytics, IoT, AI, and robotics throughout the food supply chain [UT 46][UT 49][UT 241][UT 525][UT 772][UT 1411][UT 254]. Investments in user-centric design—such as mobile and cloud-based tools, modular decision support systems, and adaptive digital twins—will democratize access to actionable insights for farmers, processors, retailers, and consumers, thereby enhancing real-time safety, quality, logistics, and personalized nutrition [UT 193][UT 1299][UT 1456][UT 769][UT 1299]. Emphasizing explainability and transparency in AI, blockchain, and federated learning implementations is critical for building trust, supporting regulatory compliance, and enabling cross-sector collaborations in both routine operations and crisis scenarios such as COVID-19 or climate-induced disruptions [UT 139][UT 55][UT 149][UT 1303][UT 1753][UT 125][UT 1545]. Broader adoption will further depend on targeted digital literacy programs, professional training, and practical guidance materials tailored to diverse stakeholders and less digitally mature environments [UT 364][UT 1521][UT 1616][UT 1407][UT 1893]. To amplify sustainability and resilience, practitioners and policymakers should prioritize green digital infrastructure, predictive analytics for waste reduction, and translational frameworks that align digital transformation with sectoral policy, risk management, and circularity objectives [UT 369][UT 560][UT 1892][UT 1906][UT 2101]. Ultimately, fostering open data ecosystems, cross-domain collaborations, and coordinated industry-regulatory partnerships will be vital in maximizing the societal and economic impact of big data and digitalization across the global food industry [UT 601][UT 1769][UT 1842][UT 313][UT 80][UT 1918].
