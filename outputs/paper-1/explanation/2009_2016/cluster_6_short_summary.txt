This cluster in food analytics is defined by the integration of advanced analytical, computational, and chemometric methodologies tailored for the detection, classification, and evaluation of chemical constituents, contaminants, and bioactive compounds within diverse food matrices, including cereals and processed foods [UT 442][UT 398][UT 2072][UT 2040]. Spanning sensor-driven and spectrum-based approaches such as raman and surface-enhanced raman spectroscopy, compact NMR, and multivariate chromatographic fingerprinting, the field emphasizes rapid, non-invasive, and multidimensional techniques for food authentication, safety, and quality assessment [UT 1613][UT 424]. Current trends indicate a decisive evolution toward the convergence of analytical chemistry and high-performance computational paradigms—marked by the use of chemometrics, machine learning, and automated computer vision systems—to achieve robust, real-time classification and detection of hazards and trace analytes in increasingly complex and heterogeneous food systems [UT 1565][UT 1736][UT 1846][UT 2080][UT 1722]. However, the field faces significant challenges, including irreproducibility and high dimensionality of spectral data, difficulty in standardizing protocols across food types, and bridging analytical outputs from chemistry, raman spectra, and informatics into a reliable, interpretable, and industry-applicable framework [UT 451][UT 1837][UT 1710][UT 2072][UT 452]. Prominent research opportunities lie in expanding and validating novel sensors, harmonizing multivariate fingerprinting strategies, fusing machine learning and chemometric models for unstructured data, and developing robust, automated pipelines tailored to real-world cereal and food applications—especially for provenance, genotype identification, and detection of water-insoluble contaminants [UT 1613][UT 441][UT 2040][UT 451]. Practically, advancing food analytics requires establishing standardized data acquisition and validation protocols, integrating rapid and non-destructive methods within automated industrial workflows, embedding user-friendly and explainable interfaces, and fostering cross-disciplinary collaboration between food chemistry, informatics, and engineering to ensure reliability, adoption, and regulatory compliance [UT 398][UT 1846][UT 396][UT 424][UT 452][UT 1710]. These steps will considerably enhance the scalability, utility, and trust in food detection systems, thereby promoting food safety, authenticity, and consumer confidence across global supply chains.
